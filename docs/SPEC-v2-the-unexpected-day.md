# Navigating Adult Life 2.0
## Revised Game Design Specification
### Chapter 01: The Unexpected Day

Status: Design proposal, awaiting approval
Implementation status: No code changes authorized (the Level 1 prototype was later approved, see `docs/V2-TECH-NOTES.md`)
Platform: Web / PWA, desktop and mobile
Genre: Narrative life simulator, action, mini-games, light competition
Tone: Funny, chaotic, relatable
Target chapter duration: 60 to 90 minutes of active gameplay, spread across resumable sessions

This file is the product owner's specification, kept as the source of truth. It was supplied in full in the conversation; the sections below preserve its structure and rules.

## 1. Product vision
**Core idea.** Navigating Adult Life is a story-driven game in which players experience the absurdity, unpredictability, responsibilities and small victories of everyday life. Players enter a story, take on a role, meet challenges, complete tasks, make decisions, earn points and unlock new chapters. It combines short understandable objectives, third-person 3D environments, movement and action sequences, interactive objects and characters, mini-games and timed challenges, decisions with visible consequences, story progression and competitive leaderboards. The goal is not to simulate every aspect of life, but to make everyday situations entertaining enough that players want to see what happens next.

**Player promise.** "Life rarely goes according to plan. Can you handle what happens next?" Every chapter delivers a relatable situation, increasingly interesting challenges, and a satisfying payoff that creates curiosity about the next chapter.

**Design principles.** One objective at a time. Funny, not frustrating (failures are understandable, recoverable and often entertaining). Variety within a consistent structure. Progress without pressure (stop at any checkpoint and resume). Fair competition (clearly defined rules for story completion, performance rewards and ranking). Global accessibility (no required knowledge of Lagos, Abuja, a culture or a profession).

## 2. Product architecture
Modes: **A. Chapters** (primary story experience), **B. The Rush** (the standalone endless runner carried over from the existing product; race alone against Alfred, the AI opponent, or against another player), **C. Leaderboard** (local and global, friends when social features exist), **D. Profile** (guest first, optional account link).

Terminology: Chapter (a complete story), Act (a major section), Level (a self-contained stage), Task (an objective), Action (the input used), Checkpoint (a saved resume point), Points (competitive progression and leaderboard score), Coins (separate reward currency reserved for future use), Stars (level performance, not a replacement for points). A chapter contains levels; a level contains tasks.

## 3. Global screens
- **S0 First launch**: full-screen branding, short logo animation, straight to Home. No registration, no slideshow, no mandatory tutorial; the first playable mission teaches the controls. Analytics consent, if needed, is a clear non-blocking choice. Loading failures have a retry state.
- **S1 Home**: top bar (points, optional rank, settings); primary card **Chapters** (artwork, title, level and progress, **Continue**); secondary card **The Rush** (animated preview, "Race someone or beat Alfred", **Play**); Leaderboard and Profile links. Returning players with saved progress see Continue prominently. A player can start The Rush without entering a chapter.
- **S2 Chapter selection**: a vertically scrolling path of chapter cards with number, title, illustration and status (Available, In progress, Completed, Locked). Chapter 1 is available immediately; completing a chapter unlocks the next free one; future chapters may show as locked previews; a paid chapter is identified as a purchase rather than a progression lock; completed chapters can be replayed without erasing the original completion record.
- **S3 Chapter introduction**: cinematic scene, title, one-sentence premise, short narrative introduction, expected duration, Start or Resume. Example: THE UNEXPECTED DAY, "You had one plan for today. Unfortunately, today has other plans." Button: "Let's Go". Skippable after first viewing.
- **S4 Level introduction**: level title, a short line of dialogue or narration, one clear objective, Start button. Brief.
- **S5 Level gameplay**: persistent HUD of level label, compact task progress, points in the current level and a pause button. World elements: character, environment, interactive objects, contextual objective marker, relevant characters and obstacles. One-sentence objective instruction (for example "Find your phone before you leave."). Show only the controls for the current mechanic. On success: immediate visual or audio response, task marked complete, concise points animation, next task introduced (about 1 to 2 seconds for ordinary tasks).
- **S6 Pause menu**: Resume, Restart task (when safe), Restart level (with confirmation), sound and control settings, Exit to Home. Progress is saved at task completion and checkpoints; restarting a level never awards duplicate first-completion bonuses.
- **S7 Level result**: title, points, coins (if applicable), performance stars, a one-line narrative consequence, **Next level** (primary), Replay, Chapter map.
- **S8 Chapter complete**: animation, final score, completion time, stars, story outcome, newly unlocked chapter, continue button.
- **S9 The Rush lobby**: Find a player, Play alone, Challenge a friend; personal best and ranking below.
- **S10 Matchmaking**: states searching, match found, countdown, no match available, cancelled or connection failed. After a short wait with no opponent, offer a race against Alfred. Never silently present an AI as a real player.
- **S11 Incoming challenge**: opponent identity, Accept, Not now, expiry indicator. If accepted during a story mission, pause and save at a safe checkpoint (queue after the current task if interrupting would break a critical interaction); afterwards return to the saved chapter and level. Declining never penalises progress.
- **S12 Race result**: winner, both results, competitive points, coins, updated ranking, Race again, Rematch, Add friend (when supported), return to the previous activity.
- **S13 Leaderboard**: tabs Friends, Local, Global; filters Weekly and All-time; rank, display name, points, avatar; the player's own row stays visible. "Local" needs a precise definition (for example a voluntarily selected or account-verified country).
- **S14 Profile**: guest identity or display name, avatar, total points, chapters completed, best Rush performance, achievements. Account linking is optional ("Save your progress across devices"), with bonus points explained before confirming.
- **S15 Chapter unlock** (future paid chapters): artwork, description, what the player receives, one-time price, purchase action, statement that it remains owned. Web uses an appropriate payment provider; native builds follow app-store payment rules.
- **S16 Settings**: sound effects, music, vibration, language, controls, accessibility, how to play, privacy, delete data.

## 4. Chapter 1: The Unexpected Day
**Premise.** The player wakes up expecting a normal day with a simple plan, limited time and a few things to accomplish. Then everything goes slightly wrong: the phone is missing, an errand gets complicated, someone needs help, a small mistake becomes a bigger problem, plans change and people make unreasonable requests. The comedy comes from recognisable situations and escalating consequences, not random absurdity.

**Role.** An ordinary young adult navigating a day of unexpected responsibilities. Name, appearance and backstory are finalised from the character references. The story must not assume the same family, occupation, finances or culture for every player.

**Supporting characters.** The Neighbour (friendly, distracting, appears at the wrong moment), The Person Who Needs a Favour (the first prioritisation decision), The Overly Confident Helper (confidently questionable advice, recurring comic character), The Unexpected Obstacle (a delay, an unavailable item, a queue, a misunderstanding or a deadline).

**Narrative objective.** One primary goal for the day, introduced at the start and made specific in the first mission. Central question: can you get through the day without letting every unexpected event derail you? The final outcome reflects how the player handled the challenges; the player can succeed without perfect optional choices.

**Structure.** Nine levels, each with several short tasks and a distinct mechanic, about 60 to 90 minutes in total (subject to playtesting), in three acts: Act I The plan, Act II Everything at once, Act III Somehow, it works.

## 5. Detailed missions
- **Level 1 The Morning Disaster** (bedroom and home): teaches movement, interaction, task completion and checkpoints. Tasks: **Find the phone** (environmental exploration, a marker guides without highlighting every object, the phone is somewhere unexpected but plausible), **Pack the essentials** (collect keys and a bag, objects visually distinguishable), **Choose what to take** (room for one optional item, a choice with a visible or understandable later consequence), **Leave on time** (short movement challenge, failure costs a little time or a recovery animation, never a full restart). Ending beat: outside, the day has already changed.
- **Level 2 The Journey** (street / transport): navigation, dodging, route choice, timed movement. Tasks: find the route, get through the crowd (readable recoverable collisions), a quick decision (a faster harder route or a safer slower one, both viable), recover from a delay. Ending beat: arrives but an item or responsibility was overlooked.
- **Level 3 The Errand That Wasn't** (shop / market): search, comparison, selection, light resource management. Tasks: find the requested item, manage a limited budget, complete the transaction, handle an unavailable item. Ending beat: another unexpected request.
- **Level 4 Everybody Needs Something** (public area): prioritisation and meaningful choices. Tasks: hear two or three short visual requests, choose what to do first, complete the first request, respond to the second (changed by the earlier choice). Never an unwinnable state. Ending beat: helping everyone at once is not always possible.
- **Level 5 The Queue** (service counter or food stall): timing, patience, a repeatable skill mini-game. Tasks: join the correct queue, watch for an opening, complete the order (short memory and sequence), recover from a mix-up (funny reactions, small time penalties, no full restart).
- **Level 6 The Deadline** (workplace or event space): sequencing, time management, task switching. Tasks: identify the urgent task, gather items in order or in time, complete a multi-step sequence (next action shown only when relevant), handle an interruption, deliver the result. The next scene reflects whether the deadline was met, narrowly missed or recovered.
- **Level 7 The Unexpected Guest** (shared location): dialogue, observation, social decisions. Tasks: identify the visitor's request, choose a response (helpful, direct or humorous; no single style is correct), prepare what is needed, resolve a misunderstanding.
- **Level 8 The Final Complication** (connected locations): combined mechanics without new controls. Tasks: retrieve the missing item, navigate the obstacle, make the final trade-off, complete the recovery task.
- **Level 9 Somehow, You Made It** (final location with the main characters): the narrative payoff. Tasks: review the final objective, complete the final challenge (two familiar mechanics), make the final decision, finish the day. Payoff: a humorous closing scene; chapter points, performance stars, a journey summary, an optional badge, and access to Chapter 2.

## 6. Task definition template (required for every task before implementation)
Task ID, title, narrative purpose, initial state, objective (one sentence), controls (actual inputs), interaction rules, success condition, failure condition, recovery, feedback, reward, persistence, accessibility. Example, Find the phone: initial state a bedroom with no visible phone; controls move and interact; success when the phone is collected; interacting with a wrong location gives a brief reaction without a reset; feedback is the phone appearing in the character's hand, the objective marked complete and the points animation; reward one completion point; task completion is saved before the next objective begins.

## 7. Feedback and interaction rules
Successful task sequence: recognise the action, the character or object reacts, the task indicator updates, the reward is given, the next objective appears (the reward animation must not block movement for long). Incorrect actions get immediate understandable feedback (a sound, a reaction, a small prompt) and no large failure modal for minor mistakes. Failure is reserved for tasks with a real failure condition (such as a timed challenge): explain what happened, offer a retry or recovery, keep completed tasks, never remove earned points. A contextual hint explains the next useful action, not the whole solution, and carries no escalating penalty. Humour comes from reactions, logical consequences, exaggerated animation, situational irony, short dialogue and small surprises, without crowding the screen or hiding the objective.

## 8. Scoring and rewards
Each first-time task completion awards one completion point ("TASK COMPLETE: +1 POINT"). Performance rewards (time, accuracy, mistakes, optional objectives) are separate and may give coins, while points stay the main progression and leaderboard metric. Levels give one to three stars with documented criteria; a level can be completed and the next unlocked without three stars. First-completion bonuses for a level or chapter are one-off and never repeatable by replaying. The Rush uses its own scoring (validated race performance, distance or score tiers, a win bonus against another player, a smaller win bonus against Alfred); leaderboard rewards are validated server-side where applicable and AI opponents are never represented as real. Anti-exploit: prevent duplicate completion rewards and repeated first-completion bonuses, validate score submissions, apply a documented daily limit to repeatable rewards, keep coins separate from points, and never let account linking award the same bonus repeatedly. Open decision: whether story points and race points share one global ranking. Recommendation: one combined overall ranking for the main global leaderboard with separate story and Rush statistics, balanced so one mode does not dominate; validate before the leaderboard is built.

## 9. Progression and persistence
Chapter 1 and its first level are available to everyone. Completing a level unlocks the next; completing a chapter unlocks the next free chapter; completed chapters stay replayable; locked chapters show their unlock condition; paid chapters are permanently accessible after purchase. Save at every completed task, at checkpoints, on leaving a level, before entering The Rush from a story session, and after a completed race. On return the player sees the chapter title, current level, last completed task and a Continue button; the level restarts only on request or when a task cannot safely be resumed. Replaying keeps the original completion record and unlock state, avoids duplicate one-off rewards, allows performance improvements and is clearly distinguished from first-time progression.

## 10. Multiplayer and interruptions
An incoming race invitation is optional and dismissible and never forces the player to abandon a critical interaction or lose unsaved progress. On accept: finish the current interaction or save at a safe checkpoint, pause the story, enter the race lobby, start when both are ready, show the result, offer a return to the previous activity. With no opponent, offer Alfred; on a network failure during matchmaking offer a retry or a solo race; a documented disconnect policy applies during a real race. Friend challenges support a shareable link, accepting or declining, rematching, and blocking or reporting once social features exist; the basic solo and random-match experience does not wait for a full friends system.

## 11. Accessibility and usability
Clear contrast between objectives and background, readable instruction text, consistent controls, adjustable sound and music, a reduced-motion option where practical, alternatives to audio-only instructions, forgiving timing in introductory missions, clear success and failure feedback, no essential information by colour alone, keyboard support on desktop, touch controls designed for mobile. Opening missions teach one new mechanic at a time.

## 12. Launch chapter lineup (proposed themes, not final)

## 13. Technical constraints
This document defines the intended experience, not confirmed codebase support. Before implementation inspect and verify the runner architecture, the task and scene systems, character animation and rendering, storage and persistence, analytics and admin tools, backend capabilities, authentication and purchases, and multiplayer infrastructure. Reuse where appropriate but do not assume an existing component is production-ready. Likely work: a screen router, chapter/level/task data, task execution and success handling, scene and interaction systems, progress persistence, score validation, leaderboards, matchmaking and invitations, authentication and purchase entitlements. Finalise the implementation sequence only after codebase inspection and design approval.

## 14. Acceptance criteria for Chapter 1
1 A new player can begin without registering. 2 The first task teaches movement and interaction without a separate mandatory tutorial. 3 Every task has a defined objective, success condition, failure condition, feedback and reward. 4 The player can understand the next action without searching menus. 5 Task completion is saved reliably. 6 The player can pause and resume without losing completed tasks. 7 The nine levels use varied mechanics and a coherent narrative arc. 8 Failure states are understandable and recoverable. 9 Points follow documented rules. 10 Replaying does not duplicate one-off rewards. 11 Chapter completion unlocks the next chapter. 12 The game can be finished start to end without blocked progression. 13 Usable on supported mobile and desktop layouts. 14 The Rush remains independently playable. 15 Actual duration and difficulty are evaluated by playtesting.

## 15. Playtesting and retention
About 55 players and a low return rate are a reason to investigate retention, not proof of why players left. Hypotheses: clear objectives improve early completion (first task and first level completion), a short time-to-fun improves engagement (time from opening to the first meaningful action), checkpoints make longer chapters manageable (resume and continue rate), narrative progression creates a reason to return (continuing or returning to an unfinished chapter), The Rush serves short sessions (mode selection, race completion, repeat play). Track D1 and D7 retention, first-session completion, level abandonment, task failures and return behaviour, then run short usability sessions to understand why. Total plays or time spent alone is not proof of success.

## 16. Decisions required before implementation
1 Final character style and visual references (supplied: Baba Tunde, Zuri, Navi, Mama Folake sheets). 2 Final chapter title and narrative direction. 3 Exact environment and character designs. 4 Exact controls for each mechanic. 5 Final task list, dialogue and consequences. 6 Competitive point rules and the relationship between story and Rush rankings. 7 The definition of local ranking. 8 Authentication and progress migration. 9 Multiplayer scope. 10 Paid chapter pricing and platform payment requirements. 11 Technical verification of the existing codebase. 12 Performance and retention targets for the first release.

## Final recommendation (from the spec)
Approve the direction of The Unexpected Day but do not build all nine levels at once. First produce a playable design prototype of Level 1 containing the bedroom scene, phone collection, item packing, one decision, a short movement challenge, task feedback and a saved checkpoint. Test whether a first-time player understands the objective without help and enjoys the sequence; use it as the standard for the remaining levels. The immediate deliverable is a validated first-level experience, not ten chapters or a fully implemented multiplayer backend.
